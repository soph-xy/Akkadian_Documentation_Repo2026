# Evaluation

Training teaches the model how to translate. Evaluation checks how well it translates Akkadian passages it has not practiced on. 

In our Akkadian translation project, we compare the English sentences produced by **ByT5** with English translations written or verified by people. We use two automatic scores, **BLEU** and **chrF++**, and then inspect actual examples for mistakes the score may miss. 

## 1\. What are we comparing?

For every passage in the test set, we need three things: Akkadian input, trusted English reference translation, and English sentences generated by our model.

**Input**: _translate Akkadian to English: ```um-ma qí-bi4-ma a-na š-ma {d}UTU```_

**Reference**: _\[Verified English translation from the held-out test dataset\]_

**Model output**: _\[English translation generated by ByT5\]_

The Akkadian line is an example of cleaned input from the project materials. The English lines are placeholders; we must use the actual reference and model output rather than inventing a translation. The marker means some of the original writing is damaged or missing.

A reference translation is not the only possible correct translation. Different people may express the same Akkadian meaning in different English words. This makes evaluation harder.

## 2\. Why use new test examples?

A model may score well on sentences it has seen during training, even if it cannot translate new ones. This is **overfitting**. To avoid it, we separate our data into different groups.
- **Training set**: The model studies these Akkadian English pairs and updates its parameters
- **Validation set**: We use these examples while developing the models, such as to select a checkpoint or decoding settings.
- **Test set**: We save these examples for the final evaluation of the chosen system.

There is another thing to be cautioned: **data leakage**. Two nearly identical lines, or two excerpts of the same tablet, can accidentally appear in both training and testing. Synthetic variations can create the same problem. We should remove duplicates and when possible keep related passages from the same source document in the same split. A trustworthy final test should use independent, human-verified references rather than synthetic translations.

## 3\. BLEU

BLEU stands for Bilingual Evaluation Understudy. It checks whether sequences of words in a model translation also appear in the reference translation. These sequences are called n-grams. A 1-gram is one word; a 2-gram is two neighboring words; a 3-gram is three, and so on.

**Example:**

Reference: The king sent a letter.

Model output: The king wrote a letter.

Both sentences share the words “the,” “king,” “a,” and “letter.” They also share the two-word groups “the king” and “a letter.” BLEU gives credit for matching word sequences, but it does not directly check whether “sent” and “wrote” express the correct meaning in context.

The usual BLEU calculation looks at word n-grams up to length four, adjusts for repeated matches, and applies a **brevity penalty** if the model produces sentences that are too short. The final score is generally shown on a 0-100 scale. Higher means more overlap with the reference, not a literal percentage of words translated correctly. BLEU is most useful when calculated over a whole test set instead of interpreting a single short sentence.

## 4\. chrF++

**chrF++** checks overlapping sequences of **characters** as well as short sequences of **words**. It uses both precision (how much of the model output matches) and recall (how much of the reference is covered). This produces a type of F-score.

**Example**: 

Reference: letters

Model word: letter

The words are not identical, but they share many character sequences, such as “let,” “ett,” and “tte.” chrF++ can recognize this partial overlap. The “++” means it also includes word 1-grams and 2-grams, rather than checking only characters.

Like BLEU, chrF++ is usually reported on a 0–100 scale. 

## 5\. Why use both scores?

BLEU and chrF++ look at the translation in different ways. BLEU emphasizes exact word sequences; chrF++ also gives partial credit for matching character patterns. Reporting both can give a more complete picture than either one alone.

**Misleading example**:

Reference: The king sent a letter

Model A: The ruler sent a letter

Model B: The king did not send a letter

Model A may be a reasonable paraphrase despite using a different word. Model B has a lot of overlapping vocabulary but reverses the meaning by adding “not.” Scores based on text overlap may undervalue good paraphrases or miss serious errors. That is why we also need human review (and most trust).

## 6\. What is the combined score?

The project outline proposes a **geometric mean** to summarize BLEU and chrF++ in one number. When both scores use the same 0–100 scale, it is calculated like this:

**Combined score = √(BLEU × chrF++).**

Example: If BLEU = 25 and chrF++ = 49, the combined score is √(25 × 49) = 35. 

The geometric mean gives a lower summary when one of the two scores is much weaker. However, it is a project-specific summary, not a universal measure of translation accuracy; BLEU and chrF++ are different metrics and their numbers are not perfectly comparable.
